# What we learned from the demos that landed, and the ones that did not

Read this the way an architect looks back at houses he built: to know what makes a room work, not to find a floor
plan to reuse. Each was designed for one client. What carries to the next demo is the reason it worked. The clients
are not named here and are never named to another client.

## The ones that landed

**A print shop that works for the defence industry.** Built by hand from one call and from reading how print shops
and their quality standard work.
- It had a name of its own, in their idea of their work: the product's journey through the stations. The name alone
  told them we understood what they manage.
- Its front page said: built from our call, from the route you described and the products you really make. Under
  it, the owner's own sentences from the call, each one opening the screen that answers it. He read his words and saw
  them taken seriously.
- One button played their day, with a clock and speeds, and the jobs moved between the stations while they watched.
- Six screens, each a place in their day. The first opened on "what needs your decision now", each alert with the
  action that closes it.
- A look drawn from their trade (ink, paper, registration marks) and real things from their world nobody asked for
  by name and everybody recognised: the barcode on a routing card, the certificate that must go out with a job.
- What followed: at the next meeting they chose to replace their existing tool, and the demo became the agreed
  reference for the real system.

**A building-management company.** The owner said customer service was their biggest mess.
- It was built on a copy of their own running dashboard, so the first thing they saw was their system with the
  missing thing now inside it.
- A world that read as a real company a month after go-live: dozens of buildings, thousands of residents, a year of
  payments, a few buildings in trouble. Every screen's depth came from that world.
- Depth on the four screens they would actually press in the meeting, chosen from their words. Flows that end in
  something: a receipt, an approval, a number that moves.
- One truth per number: the same figure on the dashboard, in the list, on the card and on the phone.
- A person per role, each landing on their own day.
- What followed: three offers and a signature in 36 hours.

**An electrical-testing company** whose technicians filled forms on paper, and whose owner had written his own
specification.
- Built one to one from his documents and three calls: every name, site and term on the screens was his.
- Two separate systems, as he thinks of them: the technician's on a phone, the office's on a desk. Plain files with
  no build step, so it opens at once on a phone in the field.
- The requirement he stressed most, working with no reception, was real: an offline switch, local save, an ordered
  upload queue. The demo was honest about the one thing that cannot run offline.
- A first version covered every screen and silently dropped six things he had asked for in words. A full re-read of
  his material found them. Read to the end, and check the demo against their sentences, not your screen list.

**An office with a project-sized offer.** The offer was a page of the demo: each priced line opened the screen it
buys. The price stopped being a number and became a thing they had just used.

## The ones that did not

- **A static page called a demo.** One page with their name. Nothing to press, so nothing to believe.
- **An app full of "לדוגמה".** Effort went to the wrapper while the screens stayed thin and every row announced it
  was fake.
- **A general system with their logo.** A service manager asked for five specific things and got a full
  organisation system where those five were scattered among screens he never mentioned. Eyal: nothing is ever a
  template.
- **Our house look on their business.** Well-made screens in one material of ours, for client after client. They
  did not sell: the client saw a product of ours and not their business.
- **AI that cannot help.** Conversations where the agent says it has no information or sends the customer to a
  person. A demo exists to show what our AI does.

## What repeats

- The client recognises themselves in the first seconds: their word for what they manage, a name from their world,
  their problem already being handled.
- Few screens, each deep, each traceable to something a person in the room said.
- Something happens when they press: the moment they described, working.
- The world behind the screens is rich and consistent enough that nobody thinks about it.
- Domain reading shows. The detail nobody asked for by name is often what convinces them we know their business.
- The look belongs to them as much as the screens do.
- Whenever a ready-made thing decided the structure or the look, the client could tell.
